# What if the Moon vanished tonight? (6-minute 'what if' video)

Status: SCRIPT FOR APPROVAL (nothing built yet).

Words: 1064 (about 5.7 min at the Kokoro am_liam pace).

## Voice-over

### 1. The hook

What if the Moon vanished tonight? Not slowly. Not someday. Gone in one second. No flash. No sound. Just an empty sky, where it used to be.

Most people think the worst part would be the dark. They are wrong. The dark is the smallest of your problems.

### 2. The first night

Tonight, the first thing you would notice is the sky. A full Moon is bright enough to walk by without a flashlight. Without it, a clear night would be darker than anything most of us have ever seen. Cities would glow. Farms and forests would turn almost black.

And the animals would notice. In Africa, a dung beetle rolls its ball of dung in a straight line, to get away from rivals. It steers by the faint pattern of light in moonlight. Take the Moon away, and the beetle loses its compass, and wanders.

On the Great Barrier Reef, corals wait for the full Moon in late spring. Then they release millions of eggs at once. Without the Moon, their most important signal would be gone.

But the dark would bring one gift. With no Moon washing out the sky, the stars would shine. The Milky Way would stretch across every clear night, like a river of light. Astronomers would be happy. For the first time, every night would be dark enough to see far, far away.

And that is only the first night.

### 3. The tides

Now look at the ocean. The Moon pulls on the water, and that pull makes the tides. Without it, the Sun would still pull on the sea, but much more weakly. By one estimate, the tides would shrink to about a third of their size.

In Canada's Bay of Fundy, the water rises more than fifteen meters. About as tall as a five story building. Without the Moon, even that giant tide would shrink.

That sounds gentle. It is not. Tide pools, mudflats, and salt marshes all live in the zone between high tide and low tide. Shrink that zone, and whole coastlines would change. Crabs, shellfish, and the birds that eat them would have to adapt, or disappear.

But the tides are not the real danger.

### 4. The wobble

Earth does not spin straight up and down. It leans, by about twenty-three degrees. That lean gives us the seasons. Summer comes when your part of the world leans toward the Sun. Winter comes when it leans away.

And the Moon helps hold that lean steady. Its gravity works like a steady hand on a spinning top.

Take the Moon away, and over a very long time, Earth's lean would start to wander. Scientists disagree on how far. Some studies say it could swing a very long way. Others say much less. Nobody is sure.

Then the seasons would stop being predictable. If Earth stood nearly upright, the seasons would almost disappear. If it tipped far over, each pole would face the Sun for months, then sit frozen in darkness for months. The weather would swing to extremes.

It would not happen in your lifetime. It would play out over many thousands of years, or longer. But it would be a slow change that life could not easily outrun.

### 5. Where the Moon came from

So why does the Moon have such a grip on us?

You see, the Moon is not a small thing. It is a little over a quarter as wide as Earth. That is huge for a moon. Mars has two small lumps of rock. Earth has a true partner.

Most scientists think the Moon was born in a crash. About four and a half billion years ago, a planet-sized object hit the young Earth. The wreckage flew into orbit, and slowly pulled together into the Moon.

Look up at it. The Moon is about three hundred eighty four thousand kilometers away. Light, the fastest thing there is, takes a little over a second to cross that gap. So you always see the Moon as it was a moment ago. The astronauts needed about three days to fly there.

And the Moon and Earth are tied together. They circle a shared point, about forty seven hundred kilometers from Earth's center, which is still inside Earth. So Earth wobbles a little every month, like a dancer being swung by a partner. Without the Moon, that dance would end.

### 6. The slow goodbye

The Moon also does something else, very slowly. It acts like a brake. Its pull on the oceans drags on Earth's spin, so our days are getting longer. About two thousandths of a second longer, every hundred years. Long ago, the Moon was much closer, and days were much shorter. By some models, only a handful of hours long.

And the Moon is leaving us. Mirrors left on its surface by astronauts let scientists bounce lasers off it, and measure the distance. The Moon drifts away about three point eight centimeters every year. About as fast as your fingernails grow.

### 7. The coincidence

Here is a strange coincidence. The Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon. And it is about four hundred times farther away. So in our sky, they look almost exactly the same size.

That is why, sometimes, the Moon covers the Sun perfectly, and day turns to night. A total solar eclipse. Without the Moon, there would be none. And even with it, they will not last. As the Moon drifts away, it will slowly look too small to cover the Sun. In hundreds of millions of years, the last total eclipse will happen.

### 8. The oldest clock

Even our calendar is built on the Moon. The word month comes from the same old root as the word Moon. The Moon takes about twenty nine and a half days to go through its phases, and for thousands of years, that was how people counted time. Take it away, and the oldest clock in the sky stops.

### 9. The verdict

So what would happen if the Moon vanished tonight? Tomorrow, the world would not end. The Sun would rise. Life would go on.

But the nights would be darker. The tides would be smaller. And the world would slowly lose its balance.

Twelve people have walked on the Moon. Their footprints are still there, because the Moon has no wind or rain to wipe them away.

We think of the Moon as a lamp in the sky. It is really an anchor.

The Moon does not just light our nights. It holds our world steady.
