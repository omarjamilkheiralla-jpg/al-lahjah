# What if the Moon vanished tonight? (6-minute day-by-day countdown)

Status: REWRITTEN SCRIPT FOR APPROVAL (nothing built yet).

Words: 980 (about 5.8 min with pauses)

## Voice-over

### HOOK

It is night. You look up, and the Moon is gone.

Not behind a cloud. Not behind a hill. Gone.

This is day one. And what happens next is worse than the dark.

### DAY ONE

Day one. The first thing you notice is the dark. Every night is now as black as a new Moon night. No more silver fields. No more light on the sea.

Across the planet, the animals feel it first. In Africa, a dung beetle has been rolling its ball in a straight line, steering by the faint pattern of moonlight. Tonight, it loses its compass. It turns in circles, lost.

Out in the ocean, the water has not changed yet. But the pull that moves it is already weaker.

### DAY TWO

Day two. The tides arrive late, and small. Without the Moon, only the Sun is pulling on the oceans. By one estimate, the tides shrink to about a third of their size.

In Canada's Bay of Fundy, the water used to rise more than fifteen meters. About as tall as a five story building. Now even that giant tide is shrinking.

Tide pools, mudflats, and salt marshes all live between high tide and low tide. That zone is shrinking. Crabs and shellfish are stranded in the wrong place. The birds that feed on them go hungry.

### ONE WEEK

One week. Nothing has collapsed. The Sun still rises. You could almost believe everything is fine.

There is even a gift. With no Moon washing out the sky, the stars shine. The Milky Way stretches across every clear night, like a river of light.

But this quiet is a trap.

### ONE MONTH

One month. And something strange has happened. There is no month. For all of human history, the Moon's cycle, about twenty nine and a half days, was how we counted time. The word month even comes from the word Moon. The oldest clock in the sky has stopped.

Spring comes to the Great Barrier Reef. For thousands of years, corals have waited for the full Moon, then released millions of eggs at once. This year, their most important signal never comes.

### ONE YEAR

One year. The seasons look the same. The days are still twenty-four hours. Most people have adjusted.

### TEN YEARS

Ten years. The tides are still small. Fishing towns that timed their boats by high water have learned new schedules. On the shore, life has shifted to follow the water. Some species of crab and shellfish are fewer. Some birds have gone looking for new beaches.

### ONE HUNDRED YEARS

One hundred years. Your great-grandchildren have never seen a Moon. Teachers tell them that a bright light once hung in the night sky, and pulled the sea. It sounds like a fairy tale.

But the biggest change has not even started. And it is not about the sky, or the sea. It is about the ground under your feet.

### THE LEAN

Earth does not spin straight up and down. It leans, by about twenty-three degrees. That lean gives us the seasons. Summer comes when your part of the world leans toward the Sun. Winter comes when it leans away.

And the Moon helps hold that lean steady. Its gravity works like a steady hand on a spinning top.

Take that hand away, and over a very long time, the lean starts to wander. Scientists disagree on how far. Some studies say it could swing a very long way. Others say much less. Nobody is sure.

### THOUSANDS OF YEARS

Thousands of years pass. Then the seasons stop being predictable. If Earth stood nearly upright, the seasons would almost disappear. If it tipped far over, each pole would face the Sun for months, then sit frozen in darkness for months. The weather would swing to extremes.

No year would be the same as the last. And life, which learned to live by the old rhythm, would struggle to keep up.

### THE REASON

So why does the Moon have such a grip on us?

You see, the Moon is not a small thing. It is a little over a quarter as wide as Earth. That is huge for a moon. Mars has two small lumps of rock. Earth has a true partner.

Most scientists think the Moon was born in a crash. About four and a half billion years ago, a planet-sized object hit the young Earth. The wreckage flew into orbit, and pulled together into the Moon.

And the two are tied together. They circle a shared point, about forty seven hundred kilometers from Earth's center, which is still inside Earth. Earth wobbles a little every month, like a dancer being swung by a partner. Without the Moon, that dance ends.

### THE TWIST

Here is the part that should worry you. The experiment has already started.

Mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts let scientists bounce lasers off it. They measured it. The Moon is drifting away, about three point eight centimeters every year. About as fast as your fingernails grow.

As it leaves, it also acts like a brake on our spin. Our days are getting longer, by about two thousandths of a second every hundred years. Right now, the Sun is about four hundred times wider than the Moon, and about four hundred times farther away. So in our sky, they look almost exactly the same size. That is why the Moon can cover the Sun perfectly, in a total solar eclipse. But as the Moon drifts away, it will slowly look too small. In hundreds of millions of years, the last total eclipse will happen.

### THE END

So what would happen if the Moon vanished tonight? The world would not end tomorrow. The Sun would rise. Life would go on.

But the nights would be darker. The tides would be smaller. And the world would slowly lose its balance.

We think of the Moon as a lamp in the sky. It is really an anchor.

The Moon does not just light our nights. It holds our world steady.
